# English Learning Profile

_Last updated: August 20, 2026_

## 1. Learner Overview

The learner practices English through spontaneous, real-time conversation and wants to become more natural and confident in spoken English, progressing toward B2/C1 fluency.

A key feature of the learner's style is active curiosity: they frequently stop to ask what a word or phrase really means, how native speakers use it, and whether two expressions are truly synonymous.

The learner benefits strongly from repetition immediately after correction rather than simply hearing the correct version.

## 2. Preferred Coaching Style

- Correct important or recurring mistakes, but do not interrupt constantly for minor slips.
- Give the natural English version briefly.
- Ask the learner to repeat the corrected sentence.
- If the learner makes a mistake during repetition, correct it and have them try again.
- Do not move on too quickly after an important correction.
- Use realistic conversation rather than isolated grammar drills.
- Explain subtle differences between words and expressions with concrete examples.
- Recycle recently learned expressions later in conversation.
- Keep explanations conversational and relatively short.
- Prioritize natural spoken English over exhaustive grammatical terminology.

### Important learner feedback

The learner explicitly noticed that when a correction is given and the conversation immediately moves on, they tend to forget the corrected wording.

Default learning loop:

**Correction → repetition → confirmation → continue**

## 3. Recurring Grammar Patterns

### A. Missing or incorrect forms of “be”

Observed:
- “we in crunch time”

Natural:
- “We’re in crunch time.”

This is an important pattern to monitor because the learner can produce the correct form once prompted.

### B. Verb tense / auxiliary problems

Observed examples:
- “I kill them” → “I killed them.”
- “we works” → “we work.”

Focus on maintaining the correct tense and subject–verb form during spontaneous speech.

### C. Prepositions and collocations

Observed:
- “on crunch time” → “in crunch time”
- Unnatural pickup phrasing → “pick her up from work”

The learner often understands the intended meaning but needs help selecting the natural English combination.

### D. Word order and phrase formation

The learner sometimes builds phrases directly from an underlying idea rather than using the conventional English expression.

Useful strategy: provide and practice the complete natural phrase instead of correcting isolated words one at a time.

## 4. Vocabulary Issues and Semantic Nuance

The learner is especially interested in what words really mean in context, not just dictionary definitions.

### “Crunch”

Three useful meanings were explored:

1. **A sound or texture**
   - “The chips have a nice crunch.”
   - “The snow crunched under my boots.”

2. **Crunch time**
   - The high-pressure period before a deadline.
   - “We’re in crunch time because the deadline is coming up.”

3. **Traffic**
   - “Crunch time” is not the normal expression for busy traffic.
   - “Rush hour” is the natural expression.

This distinction was especially important because “crunch time” and “rush hour” were initially confused.

### “On cloud nine”

Natural expression:
- “I’m on cloud nine.”

Meaning: extremely happy.

### “Pick someone up from work”

Natural expression:
- “I’m going to pick her up from work.”

This successfully replaced a less-natural construction involving “take my wife … from work.”

- “I killed them, and I need to pause the game.”
- “I’m going to pick her up from work.”

Key distinction:

- **rush hour** = busy traffic
- **crunch time** = high-pressure period before a deadline
- **behind schedule / missed deadline** = work is late or the deadline has passed

## 7. Fluency Analysis

The learner communicates successfully despite frequent hesitation.

Typical fluency characteristics:

- frequent fillers
- repeated words while searching for phrasing
- restarting sentences
- self-correction during speech
- occasional difficulty retrieving a phrase
- strong willingness to pause and ask for clarification

The main opportunity is not simply speaking faster, but becoming more automatic with common English chunks and reducing hesitation around word choice.

## 12. Coaching Protocol for Future Sessions

Start naturally. Let the learner talk about a real situation rather than beginning with a grammar lesson.

When an important mistake appears:

1. Briefly identify the problem or give a hint when self-correction is appropriate.
2. Let the learner try to correct it.
3. If needed, provide the natural version.
4. Ask the learner to repeat it.
5. Correct the repetition if necessary.
6. Have them repeat again until acceptable.
7. Confirm success.
8. Continue the conversation.
9. Recycle the same structure or expression later.

Do not simply give a correction and immediately move on.

### Prioritize

- natural spoken phrasing
- collocations
- phrasal verbs
- prepositions
- auxiliary/be forms
- tense
- subject–verb agreement
- useful vocabulary distinctions
- reducing fillers and false starts

### When explaining vocabulary

Use contrasts and concrete examples.

Example:

- **rush hour** → traffic
- **crunch time** → pressure before a deadline
- **behind schedule** → work is late
- **overtime** → working beyond normal hours

Then ask the learner to produce their own sentence.

Do not teach too many new expressions at once. One useful expression that is practiced and remembered is more valuable than ten expressions that are quickly forgotten.

## Core Principle

**Natural conversation + targeted correction + mandatory repetition + recycling.**
