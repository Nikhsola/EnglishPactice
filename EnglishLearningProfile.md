# English Learning Profile

_Last updated: September 11, 2026_

## 1. Learner Overview

The learner practices English through spontaneous, real-time conversation and wants to become more natural and confident in spoken English, progressing toward B2/C1 fluency.

A key feature of the learner's style is active curiosity: they frequently stop to ask what a word or phrase really means, how native speakers use it, and whether two expressions are truly synonymous.

The learner benefits from brief repetition after correction, but the September 11 session clarified that repeated drilling of one long corrected answer is counterproductive. The learner's primary need is sustained spontaneous speaking with targeted corrections embedded into the conversation.

## 2. Preferred Coaching Style

- Prioritize natural, extended conversation; the learner wants to spend most session time speaking rather than repeatedly reconstructing one answer.
- Correct important or recurring mistakes, but do not interrupt constantly for minor slips.
- When correcting, explicitly identify what the learner actually said, what is wrong, and the correct/natural form. Do not simply present a rewritten sentence without explaining the difference.
- Keep correction targets short enough to remember. Long model sentences cause cognitive overload and the learner may forget the beginning before reaching the end.
- Ask for one or at most two repetitions of a correction, then continue the conversation even if a minor article or small slip remains. Recycle the pattern later instead of drilling it a dozen times.
- When verb forms sound similar in voice (e.g. **try / tried**), explicitly name the tense: present, past, or future. The learner needs this semantic anchor rather than relying only on sound.
- Do not force pronoun changes that are not errors. **When I don't have...** and **When we don't have...** can both be correct depending on meaning; switching from **we** (team) to **I** (personal action) is also valid.
- Use realistic conversation rather than isolated grammar drills.
- Explain subtle differences between words and expressions with concrete, short examples.
- Recycle recently learned expressions later in conversation.
- Keep explanations conversational and short.
- Keep the conversation active after a correction; proactively ask the next relevant question rather than waiting silently.

Preferred learning loop: **Spontaneous conversation → identify key mistake(s) explicitly → short natural correction → 1–2 repetitions maximum → immediately continue conversation → recycle later.**

Observed: “we in crunch time” → “We’re in crunch time”; September 11: “they too long” → “They are too long.” Continue monitoring forms of **be** during spontaneous speech.

### B. Verb tense / auxiliary problems
Observed examples: “I kill them” → “I killed them”; “we works” → “we work.”

September 9 added evidence with hypothetical/future structures: “If I have…” + “I would…” and omission of **will** in “I’m worried that I get stuck…”.

September 11 showed confusion in oral discrimination/retrieval of **try** (present) vs. **tried** (past). The learner explicitly requested that tense be named when correcting such forms. Also observed **I start investigation** / **I making a fix**, supporting continued work on verb forms in spontaneous explanations.

### C. Prepositions and collocations
Observed:
- “on crunch time” → “in crunch time”
- “prepare to the assessment” → “prepare for the assessment”
- pickup phrasing → “pick her up from work”
- “stuck in the simple questions” → “stuck on simple questions”
- “on the pressure” → “under pressure”
- hotel phrasing needs reinforcement: **stay at/in a hotel**, not **stay on the hotel**

Preposition/collocation choice remains an active priority, but it should be corrected inside conversation rather than through prolonged drills.

### D. Word order and phrase formation
The learner sometimes builds phrases directly from an underlying idea rather than using conventional English chunks. Practice complete natural chunks, but keep correction chunks short.

September 11 examples included awkward phrases such as “the most difficult situation on my work,” “from another systems,” “for visiting beach,” and “every part of year.”

### E. State adjective vs. progressive verb form
Evidence across consecutive earlier sessions:
- “I’m worrying about the results” → “I’m worried about the results.”
- September 9: several attempts such as “I’m worrying that…” before successfully using “I’m worried that…”

Continue recycling **be worried about + noun** and **be worried that + clause**, but not necessarily as a dedicated drill.

### F. Singular/plural and determiners
September 11 showed active uncertainty about singular/plural forms and seasonal expressions. The learner clarified the distinction between **waterfall** (one) and **waterfalls** (more than one), and learned that the season is **fall**, not “falls.”

The learner can use either **in fall** or **in the fall**. Article/determiner use remains worth monitoring naturally.

## 5. Pronunciation Notes

Pronunciation feedback must remain evidence-based. Spoken production often includes fillers, repeated words, false starts, and self-corrections.

September 9 provided strong session-level evidence of difficulty distinguishing/producing **blank** versus **blink**. Recycle naturally before deciding whether this is persistent.

September 11: **try / tried** sounded sufficiently similar to the learner that they could not reliably understand which form the coach was requesting. This may be an auditory discrimination/pronunciation issue, but the learner's more important request is to explicitly label the intended tense when correcting it. Do not overdiagnose pronunciation from transcription alone.

## 6. Fluency Analysis

The learner communicates successfully and can sustain a genuine conversation across work, travel, hotels/camping, Lake Huron/Sarnia, ships, Niagara, and seasons. Frequent fillers, repeated words, restarts, and self-corrections remain present.

A major coaching insight from September 11: prolonged correction loops reduce speaking volume and frustrate the learner. The learner explicitly said they need a wider, person-to-person conversation rather than spending most of a session perfecting four to six sentences. Fluency practice should therefore dominate session time, with concise corrections inserted selectively.

Long corrected sentences create working-memory difficulty: by the end of the model sentence, the learner may forget its beginning. Use shorter chunks while still encouraging the learner to produce longer spontaneous answers in their own words.

## 10. Coaching Protocol for Future Sessions

1. Begin a natural conversation and let the learner speak at length.
2. Do not stop after every error. Select only important, recurring, or meaning-affecting errors.
3. For a selected correction, explicitly show: **you said X → natural/correct form Y → short reason**.
4. If tense is involved, explicitly say **present / past / future**.
5. Keep the repetition target short. One or two attempts maximum for most corrections.
6. Do not insist on repeating a whole long answer because of a minor slip.
7. Immediately resume the conversation after the short correction.
8. Recycle the same grammar or phrase naturally later to test spontaneous retention.
9. Do not treat legitimate personal choices such as **I** vs. **we** as errors when both fit the intended meaning.

## Core Principle

**Maximum meaningful speaking + selective explicit correction + short repetition + immediate return to conversation + later recycling.**
